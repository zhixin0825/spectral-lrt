\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Community recovery in the stochastic block model (SBM) is a basic problem in network statistics. The model assigns each node to an unknown community and makes edge probabilities depend on the communities of their endpoints \cite{holland1983}. Two common approaches use different representations of the same data. Spectral methods reduce the adjacency matrix to a small number of eigenvectors and then cluster the embedded nodes. Likelihood methods compare how well alternative community assignments explain the observed connections. The question studied here is whether the likelihood-ratio information needed for accurate classification remains available after spectral reduction, without returning to the original adjacency rows.

This question concerns the decoding rule as well as the embedding. A low-rank approximation is selected by a quadratic criterion, and standard spectral clustering applies a further Euclidean criterion through $K$-means. The oracle classifier for an SBM instead weights connections by logarithms of block probabilities and includes the contribution of absent edges. For a general connectivity matrix and unequal community sizes, its decision boundaries need not agree with nearest-center boundaries in the spectral embedding. Nevertheless, the fact that spectral reduction uses a quadratic objective does not itself imply that it discards the relevant likelihood information: a projection preserves every linear statistic whose weight vector belongs to the projected subspace.

\paragraph{Optimal recovery and likelihood refinement.}
The information-theoretic limits of the SBM are well developed. Abbe and Sandon \cite{abbe2015} characterize exact recovery in the general logarithmic-density SBM through Chernoff--Hellinger divergence and give efficient algorithms reaching that threshold. Zhou and Li \cite{zhouli2020} study the optimal error rate through Chernoff bounds and its application to community detection. We use these works to identify the oracle benchmark, while the statistical objective of the present paper is the leading-exponent form
\[
    \exp\{-(1+o(1))I\},
\]
where $I$ is the relevant pairwise Chernoff information. We do not claim the more precise optimal rate investigated by Zhou and Li.

Spectral initialization followed by likelihood refinement is also an established approach. Amini, Chen, Bickel and Levina \cite{amini2013} fit mixtures to blockwise connection counts using pseudo-likelihood and iterative updates. Gao, Ma, Zhang and Zhou \cite{gao2017} prove that a weakly consistent initializer followed by penalized local likelihood refinement achieves optimal misclassification performance under their assumptions. Zhou and Amini \cite{zhouamini2020} obtain optimal bipartite recovery using spectral initialization and pseudo-likelihood classification, and analyze a broader family of EM-type biclustering updates. In these methods the refinement uses information calculated from the original edge observations. Our algorithm imposes an additional restriction: once the selected adjacency eigenpairs have been computed, initialization, parameter updates, classification and component replacement use only those eigenpairs and quantities reconstructed from them.

\paragraph{Likelihood information in spectral representations.}
Spectral optimality is known in important special cases. Abbe, Fan, Wang and Zhong \cite{abbe2020} establish a first-order entrywise approximation for eigenvectors and show that, in the equal-sized symmetric two-community SBM at logarithmic density, an untrimmed spectral method attains the optimal recovery threshold and misclassification exponent. Their result already demonstrates that a spectral representation can retain the decisive local evidence. For sparse homogeneous SBMs with multiple, possibly unequal communities, Zhang \cite{zhang2024} gives matching upper and lower error exponents for adjacency spectral clustering with $K$-means. His spectral exponent is distinguished from the information-theoretic exponent, so a sharp analysis of a spectral algorithm does not automatically establish its statistical optimality.

Probabilistic inference based on spectral embeddings predates the present work. Suwan et al.\ \cite{suwan2016} use Gaussian-mixture limits of adjacency spectral embedding to construct an empirical Bayes prior for block membership inference. This is a relevant example of using distributional information beyond Euclidean clustering; it does not establish a sparse large-deviation equivalence between our spectral scores and the oracle edge likelihood ratio. For lower densities, Zhou and Amini \cite{zhouamini2019} provide data-driven adjacency regularization and consistency analysis in the general bipartite setting. These results supply useful tools for the extension considered in Section~4, but regularized spectral concentration by itself does not prove preservation of the oracle error exponent.

\paragraph{Our approach.}
We retain the $K$ eigenpairs of the adjacency matrix with largest absolute eigenvalues, including signal eigenvalues of either sign, and form reconstructed connection profiles. A Poisson-form working score compares each profile with candidate community profiles. Its pairwise difference has the algebraic form of a likelihood ratio. We initialize a single component at the global profile mean, then add node-profile candidates according to their gain over all nodes and refit all components after each addition. The latter criterion gives little reward for repeatedly representing an already well-explained part of the data. We also study a component-replacement step that accepts a proposed replacement only after reoptimization improves the common working objective. These choices are motivated by observed failures in which two nearby communities are represented by one intermediate profile while another community receives two components.

The interpretation as a spectral likelihood ratio has a precise algebraic basis. If $\widehat\Pi$ denotes the projector onto the retained empirical eigenspace, its signed rank-$K$ reconstruction equals $A\widehat\Pi$. With a population SBM projector, every block-constant likelihood-ratio contrast is preserved when the block matrix is full rank. Empirical projection, positivity clipping and estimated parameters introduce additional errors. In logarithmic density we control these errors locally through first-order entrywise eigenspace analysis and deterministic EM bounds. The reconstructed profiles are dependent, noninteger quantities, so the working objective is not the probability likelihood of independent Poisson observations. We therefore distinguish the numerical fitting objective from the oracle Bernoulli likelihood and state the statistical approximation required to connect them.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it specifies a growing likelihood-gain initializer and empirical spectral mixture fit using only the retained eigenpairs. Exact EM is monotone within each fixed-size fit; frozen-candidate greedy properties are stated separately for the control procedure. Second, it proves local leading-exponent recovery for the actual clipped empirical spectral profiles. This result requires a high-probability weak start, which we supply for a separate provable spectral warm-start variant. Proving the same initialization condition for the proposed growing likelihood rule remains open. Third, paired experiments on 240 graphs compare these initialization variants and diagnose severe local optima repaired by all-node likelihood gains or accepted component replacement.
