\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Community recovery in the stochastic block model (SBM) is a basic problem in network statistics. The model assigns each node to an unknown community and makes edge probabilities depend on the communities of their endpoints \cite{holland1983}. Spectral methods reduce the adjacency matrix to a small number of eigenvectors, whereas likelihood methods compare how well alternative community assignments explain the observed connections. We show that likelihood-ratio information can be decoded from the retained spectrum to achieve nearly optimal community recovery for every fixed number $K\ge2$ of communities. Writing $p=\max_{a,b}P_{ab}$, our theorem covers sparse models with $p=\Omega(\log n/n)$ and $p=o(1)$.

This question concerns the decoding rule as well as the embedding. The oracle classifier for an SBM weights connections by logarithms of block probabilities and includes the contribution of absent edges. For a general connectivity matrix and unequal community sizes, its decision boundaries need not agree with nearest-center boundaries in the spectral embedding. Nevertheless, a projection preserves every linear statistic whose weight vector belongs to the projected subspace. This gives a route from spectral compression to a likelihood decoder.

\paragraph{Optimal recovery and likelihood refinement.}
The information-theoretic limits of the SBM are well developed. Abbe and Sandon \cite{abbe2015} characterize exact recovery in the general logarithmic-density SBM through Chernoff--Hellinger divergence and give efficient algorithms reaching that threshold. Zhou and Li \cite{zhouli2020} study the optimal error rate through Chernoff bounds and its application to community detection. We use these works to identify the oracle benchmark, while the statistical objective of the present paper is the leading-exponent form
\[
    \exp\{-(1+o(1))I\},
\]
where $I$ is the relevant pairwise Chernoff information. Throughout this paper, \emph{nearly optimal} means attaining the leading oracle Chernoff exponent. We do not claim the refined multiplicative rate or a new global minimax lower bound.

Spectral initialization followed by likelihood refinement is also an established approach. Amini, Chen, Bickel and Levina \cite{amini2013} fit mixtures to blockwise connection counts using pseudo-likelihood and iterative updates. Gao, Ma, Zhang and Zhou \cite{gao2017} prove that a weakly consistent initializer followed by penalized local likelihood refinement achieves optimal misclassification performance under their assumptions. Zhou and Amini \cite{zhouamini2020} obtain optimal bipartite recovery using spectral initialization and pseudo-likelihood classification, and analyze a broader family of EM-type biclustering updates. A recent preprint of Wang \cite{wang2026vem} analyzes spectrally initialized variational EM in a general SBM, obtaining a leading Chernoff exponent while estimating parameters and reusing the original graph. In these methods the refinement uses information calculated from the original edge observations. Once our selected adjacency eigenpairs have been computed, initialization, parameter updates, classification and component replacement use only those eigenpairs and quantities reconstructed from them.

\paragraph{Likelihood information in spectral representations.}
Spectral optimality is known in important special cases. Abbe, Fan, Wang and Zhong \cite{abbe2020} establish a first-order entrywise approximation for eigenvectors and show that, in the equal-sized symmetric two-community SBM at logarithmic density, an untrimmed spectral method attains the optimal recovery threshold and misclassification exponent. Their result already demonstrates that a spectral representation can retain the decisive local evidence. For homogeneous planted-partition SBMs with multiple, possibly unequal communities, Zhang \cite{zhang2024} gives matching error exponents for high-degree trimming followed by eigenvalue-weighted spectral clustering and $K$-means. His spectral exponent is distinguished from the information-theoretic exponent, so a sharp analysis of a spectral algorithm does not automatically establish its statistical optimality.

Probabilistic inference based on spectral embeddings predates the present work. Suwan et al.\ \cite{suwan2016} use Gaussian-mixture limits of adjacency spectral embedding to construct an empirical Bayes prior for block membership inference. This is a relevant example of using distributional information beyond Euclidean clustering; it does not establish a sparse large-deviation equivalence between our spectral scores and the oracle edge likelihood ratio. For lower densities, Zhou and Amini \cite{zhouamini2019} provide data-driven adjacency regularization and consistency analysis in the general bipartite setting. These consistency results do not by themselves prove preservation of the oracle error exponent.

\paragraph{Our approach.}
We retain the $K$ eigenpairs of the adjacency matrix with largest absolute eigenvalues, including signal eigenvalues of either sign, and form positive reconstructed connection profiles $q_i$. The positivity floor is proportional to the observed spectral scale divided by $n$, so it adapts to the connection probabilities. A sparse likelihood-based working score compares these profiles with candidate community profiles. Its pairwise difference has the algebraic form of a likelihood ratio.

Initialization starts a single component at the global profile mean, adds a node-profile candidate according to its gain over all nodes, and refits every component after each addition. We then iterate a deterministic component-replacement step. Each round compares a refit of the current profiles with trials obtained by removing one component, adding the node profile with largest gain relative to the remaining components, and jointly refitting. Each trial starts with uniform weights. An improvement above the stated threshold is accepted, and the process repeats until the threshold is not met. A final EM update precedes decoding. This is an explicit iterative extension of the growing procedure. All candidate choices, fits and acceptance decisions use the same spectral profiles and working likelihood.

The general-$K$ guarantee rests on the replacement stopping certificate. If the fitted profiles leave a population community poorly represented, then with $K$ fitted components and $K$ separated population profiles, another fitted component can be removed at a sufficiently small loss. The all-node gain supplies a candidate for the omitted community, and refitting cannot reduce the trial's likelihood. Repeated accepted improvements are bounded by the available objective range. At termination, the certificate places the fitted responsibilities in a weak-recovery basin, after which local EM analysis gives the oracle error exponent. The proof does not impose weak recovery on an unspecified initializer.

The spectral interpretation has a precise algebraic basis. If $\widehat\Pi$ denotes the projector onto the retained empirical eigenspace, its signed rank-$K$ reconstruction equals $A\widehat\Pi$. With a population SBM projector, every block-constant likelihood-ratio contrast is preserved when the block matrix is full rank. Empirical projection, positivity clipping and estimated parameters introduce additional errors. We control those errors for the whole-graph empirical profiles through first-order entrywise eigenspace analysis and deterministic likelihood and EM bounds. The reconstructed profiles are dependent, noninteger quantities, so the numerical fitting objective remains a working likelihood. Its relation to the oracle Bernoulli likelihood is established by the statistical analysis.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it gives a deterministic spectral-only likelihood decoder with a complete initialization and refinement rule for every fixed $K\ge2$. Second, Theorem~\ref{thm:certified-likelihood} proves the end-to-end bound
\[
 \mathbb E\,\Mis(\widehat z,z)\le \exp\{-(1+o(1))I_n\},
\]
where $I_n$ is the minimum pairwise oracle Chernoff information. The hypotheses require positive community proportions, $P_{ab}\asymp p$, $p=\Omega(\log n/n)$, $p=o(1)$, and a uniformly conditioned rank-$K$ signal. The bound implies exact recovery when $\liminf I_n/\log n>1$. The local EM theorem and algebraic preservation results are tools for this complete recovery theorem.

Third, paired experiments on 240 graphs evaluate the original growing rule and a historical likelihood-accepted parameter-replacement procedure. They diagnose severe local optima in which nearby communities share one mixed profile while another community receives two components, and show how all-node likelihood gains or accepted replacements repair the observed failures. These archived results concern the procedures actually evaluated. They do not constitute a benchmark of the newly specified repeated-replacement Algorithm~1. Fresh checks of that algorithm verify its implementation on models with more than two communities; they do not estimate an asymptotic error exponent.
