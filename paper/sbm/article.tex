\section{Introduction}\label{sec:related-work}

The stochastic block model has a long history as a statistical model
for community structure in networks. \citet{holland1983} introduced the stochastic blockmodel
framework in 1983, in which connection probabilities are determined by the
communities of the two endpoints. Subsequent work developed likelihood
and Bayesian estimation of latent block structure~\citep{snijders1997,nowicki2001},
statistical formulations of community recovery~\citep{bickelchen2009},
and asymptotic theory for likelihood and variational
estimators~\citep{bickel2013}. A central question throughout this
literature is how accurately the unknown communities can be recovered
by a computationally feasible procedure.

\paragraph{Spectral clustering.}
A standard approach first represents each vertex by a small number of
spectral coordinates and then clusters these coordinates, typically by
$k$-means. \citet{rohe2011} studied Laplacian
spectral clustering, while \citet{sussman2012} analyzed adjacency spectral embedding.
\citet{lei2015} established consistency guarantees for
adjacency spectral clustering under sparse block models. These results
provide a statistical foundation for the familiar combination of a
spectral representation and a geometric clustering step.

Sparse graphs require additional care because unusually large or small
degrees can affect the spectral representation. Regularized spectral
procedures were studied by \citet{qin2013} and \citet{joseph2016}; \citet{llv2017}
established concentration results for regularized random graphs.
\citet{zhouamini2019} analyzed data-driven
regularization, spectral truncation and clustering in general bipartite
models, including expected degrees growing arbitrarily slowly.
\citet{chin2015} developed further spectral recovery
methods for sparse block models. Thus a logarithmic lower bound on
expected degree is not an intrinsic requirement of every spectral
approach.

\paragraph{Likelihood refinement and optimal error rates.}
Consistency alone does not determine whether the misclassification
rate is close to the best possible rate. A common way to improve an
initial clustering is to estimate its connection probabilities and
reclassify each vertex using its connections to the estimated
communities. Comparing two candidate labels is then a local
likelihood-ratio calculation, possibly with a penalty or a
pseudo-likelihood approximation. \citet{amini2013} developed scalable pseudo-likelihood methods
based on incident block counts. \citet{zhangzhou2016}
characterized minimax misclassification rates through local testing,
and \citet{gao2017} showed how penalized
local likelihood refinement can turn a weakly consistent initializer
into an estimator attaining optimal misclassification rates.

The distinction between a leading error exponent and an actual
constant-factor risk comparison matters here. \citet{zhouamini2020} combined spectral initialization with two
pseudo-likelihood classifications to obtain an oracle-optimal
biclustering rate up to constants. \citet{zhouli}
established sharp testing bounds that retain the prefactor and used
likelihood updates to obtain rate-optimal SBM recovery. These methods
show that accurate initialization need not itself be statistically
optimal: likelihood refinement can recover information that a purely
geometric assignment does not use. In these constructions the local
refinement uses observed edges or observed block counts, in addition
to the initial clustering.

\paragraph{The question addressed here.}
This development suggests a natural question: can a spectral
representation itself support the sharp recovery accuracy associated
with likelihood refinement, without returning to individual adjacency
entries? By a \emph{spectral representation} we mean the retained
leading eigenvectors together with their associated eigenvalues.
The question is not whether spectral initialization can be followed by
an edge-based likelihood update, but whether the update can instead
be computed from the retained representation.

There is already important progress on the accuracy of spectral
methods themselves. \citet{afwz} developed
entrywise eigenvector analysis with sharp community-recovery
consequences. \citet{zhang} obtained matching leading-exponent
bounds for spectral clustering through truncated spectral-row
analysis. Probabilistic classification of spectral coordinates has
also been studied, for example by \citet{suwan2016}. Our target is a constant-factor comparison
with a raw-edge likelihood oracle, rather than only a leading-exponent
comparison or a Gaussian model for the embedding coordinates.

We give an affirmative answer to this oracle-risk question for a
\emph{degree-augmented spectral representation}: one eigensystem of a
regularized adjacency matrix together with the original vertex degrees.
We call the resulting procedure \emph{spectral likelihood-ratio clustering}.
It reconstructs incident block counts, estimates block probabilities,
and updates the partition through vertexwise likelihood-ratio classification.
None of these later operations accesses individual adjacency entries.
The main theorem bounds its expected fraction of misclassified vertices
by a fixed constant times the error of an oracle that knows the true
connection probabilities and the other vertices' community labels and
classifies a vertex from its raw incident edges. The result allows
arbitrarily slowly diverging expected degrees on the positive,
well-conditioned model class specified in Section~\ref{sec:statements}.
The upper sparsity restriction and all other assumptions are stated in
Section~\ref{sec:main-result}; no logarithmic degree lower bound is
imposed.

The spectral reconstruction estimates how a vertex's connections are
distributed across communities. The observed degree supplies their
exact total, which is not in general preserved by spectral truncation.
This separation motivates the degree correction in the complete
procedure. We first explain a spectrum-only construction to make the
likelihood idea transparent, and then give the degree-augmented
procedure covered by the theorem. The result is therefore a statement
about this retained representation and clustering procedure, not a claim that
ordinary $k$-means or the preliminary uncorrected version satisfies
the same bound. Section~\ref{sec:oracle-scale} relates the oracle
error to the sharp testing exponent used by \citet{zhouli},
including the square-root prefactor, and distinguishes this comparison
from a classwise minimax statement.

\paragraph{Other recovery objectives and model extensions.}
Misclassification risk is one of several recovery criteria for SBMs.
Detection transitions were investigated by \citet{decelle2011}, with sharp two-community results by
\citet{mns2018} and
\citet{massoulie2014}; nonbacktracking methods provide
another spectral approach in very sparse networks~\citep{krzakala2013}.
Exact-recovery thresholds and efficient procedures were studied by
\citet{abh2016} and \citet{abbesandon2015}, including semidefinite approaches by
\citet{hajek2016} and \citet{aminilevina2018}. \citet{abbe2018} surveys these
recovery notions. Our average-risk guarantee concerns a different
comparison and yields exact recovery when the total oracle error
vanishes; it does not address detection at bounded expected degree.

Degree heterogeneity is a separate modeling issue. \citet{karrer2011} introduced a degree-corrected blockmodel;
related consistency, spectral and minimax results include
\citet{zhao2012}, \citet{jin2015}, and
\citet{gmzz2018}. In the ordinary SBM studied
here, preserving observed degrees corrects a reconstruction; it does
not introduce vertex-specific degree parameters into the sampling model.

Section~\ref{sec:statements} introduces the stochastic block model,
notation and oracle. Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} describes spectral likelihood-ratio clustering
and its intuition, starting with the simple spectral construction.
Section~\ref{sec:main-result} states the theorem, describes its sharp
error scale and gives an outline of the proof. Section~\ref{sec:experiments}
compares the empirical risk with the oracle as the graph size increases
and reports implementation checks and finite-sample failures. The seven
appendices contain all supporting arguments.

\section{Stochastic Block Model}\label{sec:statements}

Let $z\in[K]^n$ be the community labels and $P=P^\top$ the block
probability matrix, where $K\ge2$ is fixed. Write
$n_k=\sum_i\ind{z_i=k}$. The adjacency matrix $A$ is symmetric, has
zero diagonal, and has independent upper-triangular entries with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mean-model}
 A_{ij}\sim\Ber(P_{z_i z_j}),\qquad i<j.
\end{equation}
Put $p=\max_{k,\ell\in[K]}P_{k\ell}$. For fixed $\beta\ge1$,
$\omega>1$, and a fixed positive signal constant, assume
\begin{equation}\label{eq:structural}
 n_k\ge\frac{n}{\beta K},\qquad
 0<\frac{p}{\omega}\le P_{k\ell}\le p,\qquad
 \sigma_K(P)\ge c p.
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma_K(P)$ is the smallest singular value of the $K$-by-$K$
matrix $P$. The matrix $P/p$ may vary with $n$. For nonnegative
quantities, $f\lesssim g$ means $f\le Cg$, $f\gtrsim g$ means
$g\lesssim f$, and $f\asymp g$ means both. Unless stated otherwise,
the implicit constants may depend only on fixed $K,\beta,\omega$,
the fixed positive lower bound on $\sigma_K(P)/p$, the uniform upper
bound on $np^2$, and the fixed algorithm constants $b_0,c_0$ introduced
in Section~\ref{restore:algorithm}. They are uniform in $n$, in the
parameters within this class, and in the number of specified refits.
Local lemmas state any further dependence on fixed thresholds.
We retain explicit $c,C$ when their choice or position in a bound
matters; their values may change between occurrences. Vertex indices
are $i,j,j'$ and community indices are $k,\ell$, with $r$ used
when a third community is needed.
For any label vector $\widehat z$, use the same size convention
$n_k(\widehat z)=\sum_i\ind{\widehat z_i=k}$, so $n_k=n_k(z)$.

The misclassification proportion is
\[
 \Mis(\widehat z,z)=\frac1n\min_{\pi\in S_K}
     \sum_i\ind{\pi(\widehat z_i)\ne z_i},
\]
where $S_K$ is the set of permutations of $[K]$.
Following the classification-operator organization of \citet{zhouli},
write the row-classification formula as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sbm-oracle}
 [\mathcal L(A,P,\widehat z)]_i
 =\argmax_{k\in[K]}\sum_{j\ne i}
   \{A_{ij}\log P_{k\widehat z_j}+(1-A_{ij})\log(1-P_{k\widehat z_j})\}.
\end{equation}
Enumerate the communities in their fixed tie order, so a tie is won
by the smallest index. For the raw graph the oracle classifier outputs
$[\mathcal L(A,P,z)]_i$: it uses the true neighbor labels but not
$z_i$. An exact global size constraint is not used to infer that label.

The oracle experiment depends on the true community, not on which
vertex in that community is tested. For a representative target in
community $k$, let $b_{r}$ be its raw count of edges to community
$r$. Under $\PP_k$, these counts are independent and
\[
 b_{r}\sim\operatorname{Bin}
       \bigl(n_{r}-\ind{r=k},P_{kr}\bigr),
 \qquad r=1,\ldots,K.
\]
There are $n_k-1$ trials in the target's own community and $n_{r}$
in every other community. The self-loop is deterministically zero
under every candidate label and contributes no likelihood term; it
is not an additional Bernoulli nonedge. For $\ell\ne k$, the exact
log-likelihood ratio in favor of $k$ against $\ell$ is
\[
T_{k\ell}=\sum_{r=1}^K\Bigl[b_{r}\log\frac{P_{kr}}{P_{\ell r}}+(n_{r}-\ind{r=k}-b_{r})\log\frac{1-P_{kr}}{1-P_{\ell r}}\Bigr].
\]
For each fixed $k$, all the contrasts $T_{k\ell}$ use the same count
vector; they are not assumed independent. Different vertices in that
community give different realizations of this same experiment, so no
vertex index is needed in its distribution or risk. The average oracle
error is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oracle-risk}
 R_{\mathrm{oracle}}
 =\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{n_k}{n}\,
   \PP_k\!\left\{
     \min_{\ell<k}T_{k\ell}\le0
     \ \text{or}\ 
     \min_{\ell>k}T_{k\ell}<0
   \right\}.
\end{equation}
A minimum over an empty set is $+\infty$. The two inequalities in
\eqref{eq:oracle-risk} implement the fixed tie rule. This is the same
average error as $n^{-1}\sum_i\PP\{[\mathcal L(A,P,z)]_i\ne z_i\}$,
not a new oracle. Section~\ref{sec:oracle-scale}, specifically
\eqref{eq:oracle-scale-comparison}, relates it to the Chernoff
information and shows $R_{\mathrm{oracle}}\asymp e^{-I}$ with the
sharp prefactor included in $I$.

\section{Spectral likelihood-ratio clustering}\label{sec:algorithm}

For fixed model parameters and neighbor labels, the log-likelihood ratio
between two candidate communities depends on the incident block counts
and community sizes. Replacing raw counts by their spectral reconstructions
gives a vertexwise likelihood-ratio classifier. Fitting the block
probabilities and applying this classifier to all vertices updates the
estimated partition without reading individual edges again. We first
give the spectrum-only construction; the general procedure then adds
regularization and exact preservation of the observed row totals.

\subsection{The spectrum-only construction}\label{sec:simple}

Retain the $K$ eigenpairs of $A$ with largest absolute eigenvalues and
form
\[
 AU=U\Lambda,\qquad U^\top U=I_K,\qquad \widehat A=U\Lambda U^\top.
\]
Here $\widehat A$ is the rank-$K$ spectral reconstruction, before
any degree correction. Eigenvalues are selected by magnitude, so
informative negative ones are retained. The likelihood and parameter
fits below always exclude the diagonal. This section uses only
$(U,\Lambda)$ after the spectral calculation, not the observed
degree vector.

The vertexwise classifier in \eqref{eq:sbm-oracle} is evaluated at
its first argument: this section substitutes $\widehat A$ for $A$,
and Section~\ref{restore:algorithm} substitutes $\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}}$.
These reconstructions can be signed or asymmetric. Their classification
criterion is a working likelihood, not an assertion that their entries
are independent Bernoulli observations. The term \emph{spectral
likelihood-ratio} refers to comparisons of this criterion, not to an
exact joint likelihood for the retained eigenpairs.

\subsubsection{Block counts and likelihood-ratio classification}

Using the same first-argument convention, define the block counts by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simple-counts}
 b_{i\ell}(A,\widehat z)=\sum_{j\ne i:\widehat z_j=\ell}A_{ij}.
\end{equation}
In particular, the spectral counts are computable from the factors:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simple-factor-counts}
 b_{i\ell}(\widehat A,\widehat z)
 =U_{i\cdot}\Lambda
       \left(\sum_{j:\widehat z_j=\ell}U_{j\cdot}\right)^\top
       -\widehat A_{ii}\ind{\widehat z_i=\ell}.
\end{equation}
The expression uses only the vertex's retained spectral coordinates
and the sums of those coordinates within the estimated communities.
Thus changing the current labels changes the block counts through
these sums, without another eigendecomposition or edge lookup.
The reconstructed counts need not be integers or nonnegative. Grouping the criterion
in \eqref{eq:sbm-oracle} by the current labels gives the candidate-$k$
score
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simple-score}
 \sum_{\ell=1}^K\left\{
 b_{i\ell}(A,\widehat z)\log P_{k\ell}
 +[n_\ell(\widehat z)-\ind{\widehat z_i=\ell}-b_{i\ell}(A,\widehat z)]
       \log(1-P_{k\ell})\right\}.
\end{equation}
Thus the raw-row oracle, the simple spectral update, and the general
update below use the same classification operator with different
matrix and parameter inputs. The full nonedge term is retained.

The probability fit is just a block average. For this step write
$b_{i\ell}=b_{i\ell}(\widehat A,\widehat z)$ and compute
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-average-operator}
 \widehat P_{k\ell}\leftarrow
 \begin{cases}
 \dfrac{\sum_{i:\widehat z_i=k} b_{i\ell}}
       {n_k(\widehat z)\,n_\ell(\widehat z)},
       & k\ne\ell,\\[8pt]
 \dfrac{\sum_{i:\widehat z_i=k} b_{ik}}
       {n_k(\widehat z)\,[n_k(\widehat z)-1]},
       & k=\ell.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Both cases average the reconstructed counts over community $k$ and
then divide by the number of possible neighbors. The second excludes
self-connections. Symmetry of $\widehat A$ makes this fit symmetric. Finally, replace estimates below $n^{-2}$
by $n^{-2}$ and those above $1/2$ by $1/2$; estimates between these
bounds are unchanged. These restrictions apply only to $\widehat P$,
not to the reconstructed counts. A zero denominator invokes the
fixed fallback below.

\subsubsection{Initialization and iteration}

Normalize each nonzero row of $U$ to unit length; give a zero row a
fixed unit direction. Select a remaining row having the largest
number of remaining rows within distance $1/8$, and remove rows within
distance $1/4$ of it. Repeat for $K$ representatives and assign each
original row to its nearest representative. All ties are deterministic.
If fewer than $K$ representatives are available, use a fixed fallback
partition.

Starting with these labels $\widehat z$, compute $\widehat P$ by
\eqref{eq:block-average-operator}, apply the stated probability bounds,
and then update
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simple-fit}
 \widehat z\leftarrow\mathcal L(\widehat A,\widehat P,\widehat z).
\end{equation}
Repeat the probability fit and label update for a specified finite
positive number of rounds. Label updates are simultaneous, with the
right-hand side evaluated at the pre-update labels. Empty block-pair
sets or other undefined operations use the fixed fallback.
The probability bounds depend only on $n$, so no degree or true
probability parameter is required. For $n\ge K+1$ the interval is nonempty.

The block mean in \eqref{eq:block-average-operator}, after the stated
probability bounds are applied, maximizes
its Bernoulli working criterion, including when a reconstructed block
total is signed.
This fact does not establish convergence or monotonicity of the
simultaneous label updates. The spectrum-only procedure illustrates
the likelihood-ratio construction; \cref{restore:sbm-theorem} applies to the complete procedure next.

\subsection{The general degree-plus-spectrum algorithm}
\label{restore:algorithm}

The general clustering procedure retains raw degrees in addition to
spectral information. It regularizes high-degree vertices before computing
$\widehat A$, then corrects the row totals. The block-average fit
and vertexwise likelihood-ratio classifier are given explicitly below.
Fix sufficiently large $b_0>0$ and sufficiently small
$c_0>0$, depending only on the structural class. All choices are
deterministic, with the same fixed fallback for undefined operations.

\paragraph{Regularized adjacency spectrum.}
Let
\[
 D_i=\sum_jA_{ij},\qquad \bar D=\frac1n\sum_iD_i,
 \qquad \tau=b_0\bar D.
\]
The all-zero graph has the fixed output. Otherwise form the diagonal
matrix
\[
 W_i=\begin{cases}
 1,&D_i\le\tau,\\
 \sqrt{\tau/D_i},&D_i>\tau,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad W=\diag(W_i),\qquad \widetilde A=WAW.
\]
Retain the raw degrees $D_i$ and the $K$ eigenpairs of $\widetilde A$
with largest absolute eigenvalues:
\[
 \widetilde AU=U\Lambda,\qquad U^\top U=I_K.
\]
The decoder uses only these retained quantities; $\bar D,\tau,W$ are
recovered from the degrees. Edges between vertices below the threshold
are unchanged by the cap.

\paragraph{Original-scale spectral reconstruction and degree correction.}
The eigenpairs here belong to $\widetilde A=WAW$, so
$U\Lambda U^\top$ is a reconstruction on the regularized scale.
On the original adjacency scale, set
\[
 \widehat A=W^{-1}U\Lambda U^\top W^{-1}.
\]
For $W=I$ this reduces to $\widehat A=U\Lambda U^\top$, exactly
as in the simple version. Write $\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}}$ for its
degree-preserving, zero-diagonal correction:
\begin{equation}\label{restore:reconstruction}
 \widehat A^{\mathrm{c}}_{ii}=0,\qquad
 \widehat A^{\mathrm{c}}_{ij}=\widehat A_{ij}
      +\frac{D_i-\sum_{j'\ne i}\widehat A_{ij'}}{n-1},\quad j\ne i.
\end{equation}
Then $\sum_j\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}}_{ij}=D_i$ exactly. The entries are not clipped;
$\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}}$ can be signed and asymmetric. An isolated vertex has a
zero reconstructed row and does not halt classification of other
vertices. Everything in \eqref{restore:reconstruction} is computed
from the retained degrees and eigenpairs.

For the same labels $\widehat z$, the single count definition
\eqref{eq:simple-counts} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:count-bridge}
 \begin{aligned}
 b_{i\ell}(\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}},\widehat z)-b_{i\ell}(\widehat A,\widehat z)
 &=\frac{n_\ell(\widehat z)-\ind{\widehat z_i=\ell}}{n-1}
     \left(D_i-\sum_{j\ne i}\widehat A_{ij}\right),\\
 \sum_{\ell=1}^K b_{i\ell}(\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}},\widehat z)&=D_i.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
At fixed $\widehat z$ and $\widehat P$, the resulting change in the
candidate-$k$ versus candidate-$\ell$ log-likelihood contrast is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:score-bridge}
 \left(D_i-\sum_{j\ne i}\widehat A_{ij}\right)
 \frac1{n-1}\sum_{j\ne i}
 \log\frac{\widehat P_{k\widehat z_j}(1-\widehat P_{\ell \widehat z_j})}
               {\widehat P_{\ell \widehat z_j}(1-\widehat P_{k\widehat z_j})}.
\end{equation}
This is an identity for one update at fixed inputs, not for the final
outputs of two differently fitted procedures. An inactive cap does
not make the degree correction vanish.

\paragraph{Initialization, fitting, and classification.}
Apply the radius-count initialization of Section~\ref{sec:simple} to
this $U$. It uses $O(Kn^2)$ distance comparisons. Given current labels
$\widehat z$, recompute $b_{i\ell}=b_{i\ell}(\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}},\widehat z)$
and form the block averages in \eqref{eq:block-average-operator}.
Before applying any probability bounds, average the two directions:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block-fit}
 \widehat P\leftarrow\frac{\widehat P+\widehat P^\top}{2}.
\end{equation}
This is needed because $\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}}$ can be asymmetric.
Then replace estimates below $c_0\bar D/n$ by $c_0\bar D/n$ and
those above $1/2$ by $1/2$. The simple version's lower bound $n^{-2}$
is not used here. Only $\widehat P$ is symmetrized and restricted;
$\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}}$, its degree sums, and its classification
rows remain unchanged.
Update all labels simultaneously by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fitted-row-score}
 \widehat z\leftarrow\mathcal L(\widehat A^{\mathrm{c}},\widehat P,\widehat z).
\end{equation}
These are precisely the symmetric Bernoulli block fit and the full
edge-and-nonedge row likelihood. There is no target degree parameter
to profile in this ordinary SBM algorithm. Empty groups, empty pair
sets, or undefined operations use the fixed fallback.

Perform at least one completed fit and label update. Any finite number
of these hard refits is permitted, including finite data-dependent
stopping. The proof shows that the required operations are well-defined
on its aggregate event. No true labels, true parameters, or
leave-one-out eigensystems are algorithm inputs.

\section{Main theorem and proof outline}\label{sec:main-result}

The theorem concerns the complete spectral likelihood-ratio clustering
procedure of Section~\ref{restore:algorithm}, including its original-degree
correction. The spectrum-only construction in Section~\ref{sec:simple}
explains the likelihood-ratio principle; it is not assigned the complete
procedure's guarantee.
The model, oracle and estimator have now all been specified.

\begin{theorem}\label{restore:sbm-theorem}
Under \eqref{eq:mean-model}--\eqref{eq:structural}, suppose
\[
 np\longrightarrow\infty,\qquad np^2\le C_1<\infty.
\]
For sufficiently large fixed \(b_0\) and sufficiently small fixed
\(c_0>0\), the algorithm in
\cref{restore:algorithm}, after any finite positive number of the
specified likelihood updates, satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target}
 \E\Mis(\widehat z,z)\lesssim R_{\mathrm{oracle}}
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large \(n\). The same bound holds for finite
data-dependent stopping. The implicit constant has only the fixed
dependencies specified in Section~\ref{sec:statements}; in particular,
it is independent of \(n\) and of the number of refits.
\end{theorem}

The result permits $np=\log\log n$ and arbitrarily slower
divergence. It does not impose $np\ge c\log n$, a planted partition,
or equal expected degrees. It retains the probability-comparability
and signal conditions in \eqref{eq:structural}, as well as the upper
bound on $np^2$. Thus it is not a statement for arbitrary zero
block probabilities or vanishing relative signal separation.
The risk ratio is bounded, not asserted to tend to one. Since a
nonzero misclassification proportion is at least $1/n$, exact
recovery follows whenever $nR_{\mathrm{oracle}}\to0$.


\subsection{The sharp oracle scale and its interpretation}\label{sec:oracle-scale}

For the community-level contrast $T_{k\ell}$, define the adjusted
Chernoff information using the full community sizes:
\[
 I_{k\ell}=-\min_{0\le\alpha\le1}\sum_{r=1}^K n_{r}
 \log\!\left[P_{kr}^{1-\alpha}P_{\ell r}^\alpha
 +(1-P_{kr})^{1-\alpha}(1-P_{\ell r})^\alpha\right]
 +\frac12\log(np).
\]
Put $I=\min_{k\ne\ell}I_{k\ell}$. Following the sharp testing scale
in \citet{zhouli}, the added logarithm includes the square-root
prefactor in the exponent. We use $n_{r}$ rather than
$n_{r}-\ind{r=k}$ only in this information expression;
$T_{k\ell}$ and the oracle risk in \eqref{eq:oracle-risk} remain exact.
Adding the single missing Bernoulli factor increases the exact adjusted
Chernoff information by an amount between zero and
$-\log(1-p)=O(p)$, as shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:sharp}.
Thus $e^{-I_{k\ell}}$ is a risk scale, not an exact probability, and
this simplification changes that scale by at most a fixed factor.
The full-size expression satisfies $I_{k\ell}=I_{\ell k}$ by
replacing $\alpha$ with $1-\alpha$; the exact directional risks need
not be equal.

Under the fixed structural assumptions and $np\to\infty$,
Appendix~\ref{sec:sharp} proves, uniformly over $k\ne\ell$ and
bounded $x$,
\[
 \PP_k(T_{k\ell}<x)\asymp
 \PP_k(T_{k\ell}\le x)\asymp e^{-I_{k\ell}}.
\]
A strict defeat by any competitor forces an oracle error, and an
oracle error entails a non-strict defeat by some competitor. For
fixed $K$, \eqref{eq:oracle-risk} therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oracle-scale-comparison}
 \begin{aligned}
 R_{\mathrm{oracle}}
 &\asymp\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{n_k}{n}
              \sum_{\ell\ne k}\PP_k(T_{k\ell}\le0)\\
 &\asymp\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{n_k}{n}
              \sum_{\ell\ne k}e^{-I_{k\ell}}
 \asymp e^{-I}.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For the final comparison, a pair attaining $I$ has weight at least
$1/(\beta K)$, whereas the total weight of all pairs is $K-1$.
No equality of the pairwise exponents, and no independence of the
competing contrasts, is needed. The main conclusion can thus be written
\[
 \E\Mis(\widehat z,z)\lesssim R_{\mathrm{oracle}}\asymp e^{-I}.
\]
Here $I$ is a sharp error exponent, not the unmodified Chernoff
information or an exact negative log risk. The proof always tilts the
actual self-excluded row law. Its exact exponent can be replaced by
$I_{k\ell}$ in constant-factor bounds, but not in exact
change-of-measure identities without the one-coordinate correction.

A minimax lower bound is a statement about the worst-case risk over
a specified parameter class, as in
\citep{zhangzhou2016,zhouli,zhouamini2020}. Where such a lower bound
matches this sharp scale on the same class, a uniform oracle upper
bound yields minimax-rate optimality. That reasoning does not give
a pointwise lower bound for this estimator at every parameter value.
Accordingly, the theorem retains the upper inequality for the
algorithmic risk; the two-sided comparison above is for the oracle.

\subsection{Outline of the proof}\label{sec:proof-strategy}

\paragraph{Initialization and fitted likelihoods.}
Aggregate regularized spectral estimates give an initial partition
with $\Mis(\widehat z,z)\lesssim (np)^{-1}$. The block-fit analysis keeps its
dependence on the current error explicit: for sufficiently accurate current labels on the aggregate event,
\[
 \max_{k,\ell}|\widehat P_{k\ell}-P_{k\ell}|
 \lesssim n^{-1}+p\Mis(\widehat z,z).
\]
At a locally regular target, replacing the fitted parameters and
neighbor labels by their true values changes a reconstructed-row
likelihood contrast by $O(1+np\Mis(\widehat z,z))$. The initializer
and all specified refits share one fixed $O((np)^{-1})$ error bound,
making this score cost
bounded uniformly over all iterates. Appendix~\ref{restore:fit}
proves these statements, with one common label matching.

\paragraph{From a reconstructed score to independent incident edges.}
The full-graph eigenvectors depend on the row being classified.
For analysis only, remove the tested vertex and recompute the training
representation. A local leave-one-out comparison expresses the actual
reconstructed score as a linear statistic of the independent incident
edges, with coefficients determined by the deleted graph, plus a
bounded remainder. At the oracle tilt, projection identities bound
both the mean error and the quadratic mass of the coefficient
perturbation. No deleted graph or additional eigensystem is computed
by the algorithm. These are the roles of Appendices~\ref{restore:loo}
and~\ref{sec:probability}.

\paragraph{Sharp risk without a logarithmic degree floor.}
Uniform control of every spectral row would be unnecessarily strong
for average misclassification risk. Instead, the truncated-row bound
in Appendix~\ref{restore:tail-section} controls the expected mass of
exceptional coefficients, and the tested row pays for them only when
it actually encounters them. The sharp likelihood comparison in
Appendix~\ref{sec:sharp} retains the prefactor already included in
$I$. The polynomial and exponential aggregate bounds in
Appendices~\ref{sec:sparse-extension} and~\ref{sec:high-range} cover
complementary degree ranges; their combination is not a logarithmic
lower-degree assumption. Averaging the local bounds and paying the
exceptional events at the oracle scale completes the theorem.

All seven proof appendices remain part of this ordinary-SBM article.
No degree-corrected-SBM extension or companion note is needed for its
main theorem.
